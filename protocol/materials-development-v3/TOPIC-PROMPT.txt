Write one standalone, neutral research question. The supplied article must directly contribute evidence toward answering it, but the question must not be a disguised request to reproduce that article. A reader should be able to compare evidence from different studies addressing the same scientific problem.

1. Identify ONE principal research objective from the work actually performed, not a speculative application or a striking result. Ask about the relationship, effect, performance, or feasibility investigated. Do not assume a benefit, harm, association, mechanism, superiority, successful prevention, or result direction. Observational associations must not become causal intervention effects.

2. Choose a synthesis-level focus. For an evaluation of a particular biomarker, material, device, or experimental/training model, use its immediate scientifically meaningful class and the purpose investigated when that class is supported by the source. The particular object can then supply one example of evidence within that class. Do not require its exact molecular identifier, branded formulation, apparatus, anatomical implementation, and all its outcomes together. For a mechanistic question where a specific entity is essential to the relationship, retain it instead of replacing the relationship with a different topic. Do not broaden to the entire disease, all possible treatments, all biomarkers, or all scientific methods merely to find more articles. A scientifically coherent class is not an arbitrary list of unrelated objects.

3. Preserve distinctions that matter to interpretation: condition, relevant population or biological system, and purpose or outcome domain. Distinguish patient observations, animal studies, isolated cells, and computational analyses. Do not imply clinical efficacy from laboratory experiments. A class of experimental models may include different model implementations without claiming that they are equivalent. If the source combines patient and laboratory work, select one coherent principal focus or explicitly distinguish the settings; do not concatenate every experiment, mechanism, assay, and secondary endpoint.

Do not convert a hypothesis, background motivation, or future proposal into something actually tested.

Neutrality and scientific fidelity take priority over breadth.
